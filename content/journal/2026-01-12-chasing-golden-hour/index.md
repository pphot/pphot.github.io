Golden hour is not an hour. It is the twenty minutes before a place loses its edges, when the sky gives up its white and everything underneath turns the colour of amber. I spent most of my twenties walking past it.

{{< figure src="/img/gallery/golden-hour-03.webp" alt="Cliffs lit from the side by low evening light" size="full" >}}

## Why it keeps working

The appeal is not really the light. It is proof that you stood in a specific place, in a specific year, and noticed. A photograph taken at noon is documentation. A photograph taken at golden hour is a decision.

{{% callout type="info" title="The one rule that helped" %}}
Set the tripod up while the scene is still dark. Every good frame of the evening came before the light looked worth shooting.
{%/ callout %}}

## The part nobody photographs

The pictures are the reason to go. They are not the reason to keep going. That part is the walk back: ten minutes of dark road, the camera heavy in your pocket, nothing left to shoot.

{{< imagepair left="/img/gallery/golden-hour-02.webp" left_alt="Fog moving through the stacks at first light" right="/img/gallery/golden-hour-07.webp" right_alt="A canyon road at dusk" caption="Two hours, two directions, same walk back." >}}

{{% quote cite="Robert Frank" %}}
There is always one more time.
{%/ quote %}}

## What actually changed my routine

I stopped planning the shot and started planning the walk. Once the destination stops mattering, you start noticing the things you would have driven straight past. Those are the ones worth a frame.

{{< figure src="/img/gallery/golden-hour-06.webp" alt="A row of lit candles on a dark table" caption="The one subject that never needs the sky." >}}

That is the whole trick, and it took me longer than it should have.
